Cutting out the middlemen. Putting Ugandan farmers in front of global buyers — directly.

Kawa is a coffee marketplace where farmers, cafés, and suppliers post their coffee and connect with buyers worldwide. Sellers create an account, upload their inventory, set their own prices, and become instantly visible — without the chain of intermediaries that usually sits between a Ugandan farmer and the global market.

I designed the full UX and UI — the research framing, user flows, interface, and design system that holds it together, across both the Android app and the marketing website.

The Problem

A real structural gap in the market

Uganda is one of the world's major coffee producers, powered overwhelmingly by smallholders — roughly 1.8 million smallholder farmers account for the vast majority of the country's output. Yet those farmers capture only a small fraction of what their coffee is ultimately worth.

Between the farm gate and the global buyer sits a long chain of intermediaries, each absorbing part of the margin. Packaged Ugandan coffee can sell for several dollars a kilo on the world market while the farmer who grew it sees only a fraction of that — with little pricing transparency, limited direct buyer contact, and almost no digital shopfront to reach a roaster in Europe or North America.

"The gap Kawa is designed to close is real. But the design challenge underneath the mission is specific and hard."

01
Design for real constraints
Many core sellers are rural farmers using a marketplace app for the first time, on entry-level Android phones over patchy mobile data. Simplicity isn't a preference — it's adoption or abandonment.
02
Serve both sides of a marketplace
A farmer posting a sack of beans and an international roaster sourcing premium lots need different things from the same product. Selling had to be effortless and buying had to be trustworthy.
03
Build trust across a distance
A buyer thousands of miles away needs enough signal — clear product details, prices, and seller profiles — to feel confident trading with someone they've never met. Trust is the real currency of cross-border direct trade.

Users

Two sides, one marketplace

Kawa serves two primary groups with a third in support. Each has a distinct job to be done — and the design had to serve all of them without compromise.

🌱
Sellers
Farmers, cafés, and suppliers. They need to register, upload inventory, set prices, and present their coffee well. Ease and confidence matter more than advanced features.
☕
Buyers
Roasters, cafés, and coffee enthusiasts. They need to browse, evaluate quality and price, judge a seller's credibility, and make direct contact with confidence.
🌍
The wider coffee community
The Feed frames Kawa not just as a transactional catalog, but as a place where coffee people discover, connect, and share — giving the product a living, social quality.

Goals

What the design had to achieve

Make posting coffee for sale effortless — simple enough that a first-time, non-technical user can do it in minutes.
Make discovering and trusting a seller easy — so a distant buyer will confidently reach out directly.
Keep the app light and accessible — fast on low-end devices and forgiving on inconsistent mobile data connections.
Communicate the pricing model clearly — free during the testing period, then paid — without creating friction or confusion at onboarding.
Express a brand that feels proudly Ugandan, warm, and credible — authentic to its roots while professional enough to earn international buyer trust.

Process

Designing for the real context

"Accessibility here isn't a nicety — it's the difference between a farmer adopting the app or abandoning it."

1
Designing for the real user and the real device
I approached the UI on the assumption of a first-time user on a budget Android device over inconsistent data. That pushed every decision toward simplicity: large clear targets, minimal steps, plain language, and a lightweight interface that doesn't depend on heavy media or constant connectivity.
2
Structuring a two-sided marketplace
I organized the app around a small set of clear surfaces — a Feed for discovery, Profiles that establish seller identity, Products for listings with prices and details, and Pricing for the app's own plans. The architecture had to let a seller move quickly from "I have coffee to sell" to a live visible listing, while letting a buyer move from discovery to direct contact with as little friction as possible.
3
Reducing the seller journey to its essentials
The core value of Kawa is that anyone can list their coffee. So the seller flow — create an account, upload inventory, set a price, add details, publish — was designed to be short, guided, and hard to get wrong. Every extra step or moment of confusion is a farmer lost.
4
Designing for trust on the buyer side
Because buyers and sellers may be continents apart, I focused buyer-facing screens on the signals that build confidence: clear product details, transparent prices, and seller profiles that make the person behind the coffee feel real and credible. The goal was to make direct, intermediary-free trade feel safe.
5
A brand rooted in place
Kawa is "made in Uganda," and the design leans into that rather than hiding it. The visual language was built to feel warm, authentic, and proudly local while still reading as trustworthy and professional to an international buyer evaluating a purchase.

Key Screens & Decisions

Where the design does the work

Discovery
The Feed
The entry point and the heart of the marketplace — a browseable stream of coffee posts that makes the catalog feel alive and social rather than a static directory. Designed to surface quality coffee quickly and pull buyers toward sellers, while giving the product a living community feel.
Listings
Product listings — where a sale is won or lost
Each listing was designed to present the essentials a buyer needs — coffee details, price, and seller — clearly and scannably, so a buyer can evaluate an option at a glance and a seller can present their coffee with pride. Information density was calibrated so it loads fast and reads without effort.
Trust
Seller profiles — the trust anchor
A profile turns an anonymous listing into a credible counterpart, giving buyers the context they need to reach out across a long distance. The profile surface was the single most important trust-building element in the product — a buyer who believes in the seller will follow through where a buyer who doesn't will bounce.
Seller Flow
Create listing — the most important flow in the product
Designed as a short, guided sequence so a first-time, non-technical user can get their coffee online and visible in minutes. Every decision — field order, copy tone, step count — was made to reduce anxiety and forward momentum. If this flow feels hard, the whole product fails for its primary users.
Onboarding
Pricing & onboarding — earning trust before asking for anything
Communicating the free-testing-period model and what happens afterward, clearly enough that a new user understands the value before being asked for anything. Pricing transparency at onboarding is a conversion lever — uncertainty about cost kills signups before the product gets a chance to prove itself.
Web
Marketing website at kawacoffee.app
A focused landing experience that explains the value to both farmers and buyers, drives Android downloads, and answers the key questions — cost, devices supported, availability — through a simple FAQ. The site serves as the top-of-funnel proof layer: the place a cautious user goes before committing to a download.

A complete UX/UI design — research framing, user flows, interface, and design system — for a two-sided coffee marketplace spanning a mobile app and a marketing website. A live Android product and website built from those designs, currently free during an introductory testing period.

A product designed specifically for its context: light enough for entry-level devices and modest connectivity, simple enough for first-time sellers, and credible enough for international buyers to trust a farmer they've never met.

A note on the problem framing: The facts in this case study are real and sourced — Uganda has ~1.8M smallholder coffee farmers producing the vast majority of national output; farmers capture only a fraction of the ~$4.5/kg global price because of multiple intermediaries; the UCDA is actively pushing to cut middlemen and open direct market access. The design problem is as real as the mission.

What I'd Explore Next

Room to grow

🧪
Field usability testing
Watching first-time sellers complete the listing flow on their own devices in-country. The highest-value research this product could run.
🗣️
Localization & language
Supporting local Ugandan languages alongside English to widen adoption among rural sellers who are most comfortable in their first language.
📶
Offline-tolerant patterns
Letting a seller draft a listing without a stable connection and sync when they're back online — critical for users with inconsistent rural data.
🔒
Trust & safety features
Ratings, verification, or messaging patterns that further de-risk cross-border direct trade as the marketplace grows and volume increases.
